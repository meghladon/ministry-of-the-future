The Ministry of the Future ↳ Australia An experiment in collective imagination

The Ministry of the Future is a small project testing whether vAustralia — our adaptation of Polis, the deliberation tool used by vTaiwan to resolve real political deadlocks in Taipei — could help Australians think together about what this country should become. We're running a dry run. You're invited to try it.

Why this exists

Democracy is slipping
almost everywhere.

For the first time in more than twenty years, the world has more autocracies than democracies — 91 to 88. Nearly a quarter of all countries are now in active democratic backsliding, including, for the first time in modern history, large and stable Western democracies like the United States, the United Kingdom, and Italy. Polarisation is deepening in roughly a quarter of all countries on earth.1

Australia is, for now, an unusual exception. A record 74% of Australians say democracy is preferable to any other form of government — the highest figure the Lowy Institute has ever recorded. But 70% also believe social media is making democracy worse, and trust in our political parties has been in long decline.2 We are holding the line — but we're holding it on infrastructure that was built before the internet, and we're holding it while every other comparable country slides past us in the wrong direction.

The same digital tools that have hollowed out public discourse over the past two decades can be turned the other way. Polis, the deliberation method developed by a Seattle team and famously deployed by Audrey Tang's team in Taiwan, has been used to resolve legislative deadlocks across more than a dozen countries — from Uber regulation in Taipei to climate policy in Austria, fintech rules in Britain, and a constitutional consultation for an AI system. The pattern in every case is the same: people who appeared to be at war with each other turned out to share more than the loudest voices suggested. The tool found the agreement that the noise was hiding.

Field Notes / 01

How they did it
in Taiwan.

The method we use isn't ours. It was forged in Taipei after the 2014 Sunflower Movement, by a civic-tech crew called g0v and a digital minister named Audrey Tang. Over the past decade it has shaped real legislation — on ride-sharing, alcohol sales, fintech, telemedicine — with a documented government action rate of around 80%. Here's how it works, and why it works.

The core insight is almost embarrassingly simple. On most contested issues, people aren't actually as divided as the loudest voices suggest. They're divided on some things and quietly agreed on others — but the agreed parts are invisible because nobody's shouting about them.

The Taiwanese tool, called Polis, uses a clustering algorithm to map opinion groups in real time, then surfaces the statements that cross between them. These are called bridging statements. They're the ideas that earn agreement from people who otherwise disagree about everything. They're rare. They're how consensus actually works.

What gets hidden, on purpose, is the divisive stuff. The platform doesn't reward outrage. It rewards finding the thing your opponent can also live with.

Fig. 01 — How bridging works↓

Each dot is a participant. The two ellipses are opinion clusters discovered by the algorithm. A statement only one cluster agrees with (top) gets demoted. A statement both clusters agree with (bottom) gets surfaced — that's the bridge. Consensus is built from the bottom up, not negotiated top-down.

Fig. 02 — The vTaiwan process, end to end

Polis is one stage of four. The platform handles the opinion-mapping. Humans — facilitators, stakeholders, civil servants — handle everything else. The Ministry of the Future is our version of the human work around the tool.

Case 01 / 2015 · The Uber question

When Uber arrived in Taiwan in 2015 it set off the same fight it set off everywhere else: taxi drivers losing 30% of their income, regulators baffled, customers worried about safety, the company itself refusing to engage. No ministry wanted to touch it. The Ministry of Transport saw Uber drivers as criminals; the Ministry of Economic Affairs didn't think they were e-commerce; the Ministry of Finance had never heard of "the sharing economy."

So vTaiwan ran a Polis round instead, and split participants into four stakeholder groups: Uber drivers, taxi drivers, Uber passengers, taxi passengers. Within hours the four collapsed into two — pro-Uber and anti-Uber, screaming at each other in opinion-space. Then something strange happened. As people kept proposing statements, trying to win more agreement, they started writing things both sides could live with. Insurance. Registration. Fair taxation. A level playing field. Seven statements eventually crossed the 80% bridging threshold across both camps.

Audrey Tang took those seven statements into a face-to-face meeting with Uber, the taxi associations, and government — live-streamed to 1,875 viewers. With clear public consensus already on the table, Uber agreed to obtain professional licenses for its drivers, release its insurance policies, and pay transport taxes. The Ministry of Transportation amended the regulations along the lines vTaiwan had produced. Nobody walked in with that compromise. The bridging algorithm found it.

A 13-year deadlock, broken in three weeks.

Taiwan banned online alcohol sales in 2003. By 2012 the Ministry of Finance wanted to lift the ban, but every attempt to write new rules collapsed under the weight of the people in the room: retailers wanted no restrictions, e-commerce platforms wanted clarity, parents wanted protection, public-health groups wanted prohibition. They had been talking past each other for years. The regulation went nowhere.

In March 2016, the question went to vTaiwan. About 450 citizens proposed and voted on solutions over a few weeks. What emerged from the cluster map wasn't yes-or-no. It was yes, but — a conditional permission with specific guardrails: age verification at purchase, restricted delivery hours, identity checks at handover. None of those conditions were anyone's opening position. They were the bridging statements — the things every group could agree on once they stopped trying to win.

The new regulation reflected those conditions almost exactly. This is the case that's harder to tell than the Uber one, but it's the more important one. Uber proved Polis could find consensus on a polarised issue. The alcohol case proved it could produce a novel compromise — one that nobody walked in carrying, that emerged from the structure of agreement itself. That's the part of the Taiwanese method we're trying to bring here.

The Ministry of the Future is a small attempt to test whether the human work around this tool — the kind of work that made Taiwan's experiment matter — could be adapted for Australia. The first step is finding out if the tool itself resonates with people here.

The dry run / Round 01

The first round
is live.

We've put one earnest question into vAustralia — finish the sentence: in an ideal world… — with five seed statements about water, neighbours, trains, cities, and housing. Vote on them. Watch the cluster map fill up as other people vote. See what we secretly agree on.

This is a dry run. We're testing whether the tool resonates here, whether the bridging algorithm finds anything interesting at small scale, and whether real Australians want to think about questions like this. The answers will shape what we do next.

Field Notes / 02

Built slowly,
in public.

The Ministry of the Future is being built openly. Here's what we've done, what we're working on next, and what we're thinking about. Add your own suggestions at the bottom — the same way the tool itself works: collectively.

What we've done

  1. 01
    The first vAustralia prototype

    A standalone HTML build of the deliberation tool with simulated participants voting on Australian infrastructure statements. Local-first, no backend.

  2. 02
    Real multi-user voting

    Replaced the local prototype with a Supabase-backed version, so multiple people in different places can vote on the same round simultaneously.

  3. 03
    The Ministry of the Future site

    This page. The project's public face, the Taiwan story, the case for why this work matters.

  4. 04
    Stakeholder & systems map

    A concentric-circle map of the Australian civic-tech and deliberative-democracy ecosystem — who's already doing this work, who could be allies, who sets the context.

  5. 05
    vAustralia v1.1 — the dry run

    The current round. One earnest question, five seed statements, fully anonymous, single-round Polis-faithful. Live now. Testing whether the tool resonates.

What's next

  1. →
    Crowdsourcing a regional festival

    Use vAustralia to help shape a small festival in central Victoria — music, water slides, the works. The kind of festival that could only exist because the people coming to it had a hand in shaping it. The first real-world use of the tool, and a deliberately joyful one.

  2. →
    A trial at a tax reform conference

    We're in early conversations with the Australian network of the School of Moral Ambition about trialling vAustralia at a tax reform policy conference later this year. Tax reform is one of the most chronically deadlocked policy areas in Australian politics — exactly the kind of place where the bridging method earned its reputation in Taiwan.

What we're thinking about

  1. ?
    Larger infrastructure deliberations

    High-speed rail, water security, energy grids, housing supply — the chronically deadlocked questions Australia famously can't decide on. Could vAustralia help find the bridging statements hiding underneath?

  2. ?
    Improving the platform itself

    Better clustering algorithms, a moderation queue for participant-proposed statements, mobile-friendly cluster maps, multi-round support, exportable session artefacts.

  3. ?
    Partnering with local councils

    Running rounds with the kinds of organisations that already have a mandate to consult their communities — and that could use a better tool for it than a paper survey or a town-hall meeting.

  4. ?
    Joining the global Polis community

    Connecting with the Computational Democracy Project, comparing notes with Decidim and Make.org, contributing back to the open-source ecosystem we're building on.
