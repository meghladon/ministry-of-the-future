The Ministry of the Future ↳ Australia An experiment in collective imagination

The Ministry of the Future is a project testing whether vAustralia, an adaptation of Polis, could help Australians think together about what this country should become. Polis is the deliberation tool used by vTaiwan to resolve real political deadlocks in Taipei. We're running a dry run. You're invited to try it.

The V‑Dem Institute's latest Democracy Report calls the last two decades "the third wave of autocratization". In 2024, for the first time in more than twenty years, autocracies outnumbered democracies. Freedom of expression is deteriorating in nearly a quarter of countries, a new record. Polarisation is deepening in roughly a quarter of all countries on earth.1

Australia is, for now, an unusual exception. A record 74% of Australians say democracy is preferable to any other form of government, the highest figure the Lowy Institute has ever recorded. But 70% also believe social media is making democracy worse, and trust in our political parties has been in long decline.2 We are holding the line. But we're holding it on infrastructure that was built before the internet, and we're holding it while every other comparable country slides past us in the wrong direction.

The same digital tools that have hollowed out public discourse over the past two decades can be turned the other way. Polis, the deliberation method developed by a Seattle team and famously deployed by Audrey Tang's team in Taiwan, has been used to resolve legislative deadlocks across more than a dozen countries, from Uber regulation in Taipei to climate policy in Austria, fintech rules in Britain, and a constitutional consultation for an AI system. The pattern in every case is the same. People who appeared to be at war with each other turned out to share more than the loudest voices suggested. The tool found the agreement that the noise was hiding.

Field Notes / 01

How they did it
in Taiwan.

The method we use isn't ours. It was forged in Taipei after the 2014 Sunflower Movement, by a civic-tech crew called g0v and a digital minister named Audrey Tang. Over the past decade it has shaped real legislation, on ride-sharing, alcohol sales, fintech, and telemedicine, with a documented government action rate of around 80%. Here's how it works, and why it works.

The core insight is almost embarrassingly simple. On most contested issues, people aren't actually as divided as the loudest voices suggest. They're divided on some things and quietly agreed on others. The agreed parts are invisible because nobody's shouting about them.

The Taiwanese tool, called Polis, uses a clustering algorithm to map opinion groups in real time, then surfaces the statements that cross between them. These are called bridging statements. They're the ideas that earn agreement from people who otherwise disagree about everything. They're rare. They're how consensus actually works.

What gets hidden, on purpose, is the divisive stuff. The platform doesn't reward outrage. It rewards finding the thing your opponent can also live with.

Fig. 01 · How bridging works↓

Each dot is a participant. The two ellipses are opinion clusters discovered by the algorithm. A statement only one cluster agrees with (top) gets demoted. A statement both clusters agree with (bottom) gets surfaced. That's the bridge. Consensus is built from the bottom up, not negotiated top-down.

Fig. 02 · The vTaiwan process, end to end

Polis is one stage of four. The platform handles the opinion-mapping. Humans, facilitators, stakeholders, civil servants, handle everything else. The Ministry of the Future is our version of the human work around the tool.

Fig. 03 · The four stages, unpacked

vTaiwan separates the work into four discrete stages, each with its own tools, participants, and outputs. Pol.is is one stage of four. The rest is human work — and most of it isn't online.

01
Proposal
offline · weekly

A government agency agrees to be accountable for an issue. A facilitator takes charge. The community maps stakeholders and gathers facts.

  • g0v Wednesday mini-hackathons
  • Stakeholder discovery
  • Background documents and an open dictionary of terms
  • Rolling questionnaires sent through stakeholder networks
02
Opinion
online · ~1 month

Participants vote, propose, and cluster. The algorithm finds the bridging statements — the ideas both camps can live with — and demotes the divisive ones.

  • Pol.is for opinion mapping and clustering
  • Discourse for tagged Q&A; ministries reply within 7 days
  • Anyone can join — not just citizens, not just residents
  • Outputs: a raw report and a curated second-hand report
03
Reflection
offline · live-streamed

A face-to-face consultation with the stakeholders the bridging map surfaced. Live-streamed on YouTube; the chat feeds the room. Reports become the record.

  • Pre-meeting between facilitator and the agency
  • Stakeholder presentations + facilitator's real-time digital notes
  • Public livestream with chat curated into the discussion
  • Transcript becomes the input to ratification
04
Ratification
offline · final

The agency formalises a "rough consensus": a guideline, a policy statement, or a draft bill. If they decline, they must say why, point by point.

  • Community + agency review the full record
  • Outcomes: regulation, guideline, or reasoned rejection
  • Each ministry has a Participation Officer (since 2017) responsible for the response
  • ~80% of vTaiwan rounds have led to government action

Case 01 / 2015 · The Uber question

When Uber arrived in Taiwan in 2015 it set off the same fight it set off everywhere else: taxi drivers losing 30% of their income, regulators baffled, customers worried about safety, the company itself refusing to engage. No ministry wanted to touch it. The Ministry of Transport saw Uber drivers as criminals; the Ministry of Economic Affairs didn't think they were e-commerce; the Ministry of Finance had never heard of "the sharing economy."

So vTaiwan ran a Polis round instead, and split participants into four stakeholder groups: Uber drivers, taxi drivers, Uber passengers, taxi passengers. Within hours the four collapsed into two camps, pro-Uber and anti-Uber, screaming at each other in opinion-space. Then something strange happened. As people kept proposing statements, trying to win more agreement, they started writing things both sides could live with. Insurance. Registration. Fair taxation. A level playing field. Seven statements eventually crossed the 80% bridging threshold across both camps.

Audrey Tang took those seven statements into a face-to-face meeting with Uber, the taxi associations, and government, live-streamed to 1,875 viewers. With clear public consensus already on the table, Uber agreed to obtain professional licenses for its drivers, release its insurance policies, and pay transport taxes. The Ministry of Transportation amended the regulations along the lines vTaiwan had produced. Nobody walked in with that compromise. The bridging algorithm found it.

A 13-year deadlock, broken in three weeks.

Case 02 / 2016 · The alcohol question

Taiwan banned online alcohol sales in 2003. By 2012 the Ministry of Finance wanted to lift the ban, but every attempt to write new rules collapsed under the weight of the people in the room: retailers wanted no restrictions, e-commerce platforms wanted clarity, parents wanted protection, public-health groups wanted prohibition. They had been talking past each other for years. The regulation went nowhere.

In March 2016, the question went to vTaiwan. About 450 citizens proposed and voted on solutions over a few weeks. What emerged from the cluster map wasn't yes-or-no. It was yes, but: a conditional permission with specific guardrails. Age verification at purchase. Restricted delivery hours. Identity checks at handover. None of those conditions were anyone's opening position. They were the bridging statements, the things every group could agree on once they stopped trying to win.

The new regulation reflected those conditions almost exactly. This is the case that's harder to tell than the Uber one, but it's the more important one. Uber proved Polis could find consensus on a polarised issue. The alcohol case proved it could produce a novel compromise, one that nobody walked in carrying, that emerged from the structure of agreement itself. That's the part of the Taiwanese method we're trying to bring here.

The Ministry of the Future is an experiment in whether the human work around this tool, the kind of work that made Taiwan's deliberations matter, could be adapted for Australia. The first step is finding out if the tool itself resonates with people here.

The tax round

A real question,
asked the Taiwan way.

In partnership with the Australian network of the School of Moral Ambition, we're running a vTaiwan-style round on one of the most chronically deadlocked questions in Australian politics: how this country taxes wealth, capital, and intergenerational transfers. This is exactly the kind of fight the bridging method was built for — many camps, decades of stalemate, and a quiet majority that probably agrees on more than the loudest voices suggest.

The question · Round 02
How should Australia tax wealth, capital, and intergenerational transfers over the next decade?

Scoped narrowly, on purpose. Taiwan's Uber round didn't ask "what's the future of transport." It asked one question with clear stakeholders. A cluster map only finds bridges when the disagreement is specific.

Methodology · vTaiwan, since 2015 pol.is ↗ · Computational Democracy Project ↗

↳ What you're doing right now, and what happens after

Step 01 · You
Vote & propose

Agree, disagree, or pass on each statement. Add your own. The algorithm sorts everyone into opinion clusters and finds what bridges them.

Step 02 · Pol.is
Cluster & bridge

Real-time PCA + k-means. The map updates as votes come in. Statements that earn agreement across all clusters get surfaced; divisive ones get demoted.

Step 03 · Conference
Reflection

The bridging statements go into a face-to-face session at the School of Moral Ambition tax reform conference. Stakeholders, economists, public servants, in one room.

Step 04 · Output
Public report

A raw report and a curated read-back, published openly. A statement of where the bridges are, what the room agreed to, and what's still contested.

Tax is supposed to be the topic that breaks deliberation. It's polarised, it's technical, it's tied up with identity and class. That's exactly the kind of question Taiwan's bridging method earned its reputation on. We don't know if it will work here. That's what the round is for.

    A standalone HTML build of the deliberation tool with simulated participants voting on Australian infrastructure statements. Local-first, no backend.

  4. 04

  6. 06

    Running on hosted pol.is, embedded above. In partnership with the Australian network of the School of Moral Ambition. The first real-stakes round, on the most chronically deadlocked policy question in Australian politics.

What's next

  1. →
    Crowdsourcing a regional festival

    Use vAustralia to help shape a small festival in central Victoria: music, water slides, the works. The kind of festival that could only exist because the people coming to it had a hand in shaping it. The first real-world use of the tool, and a deliberately joyful one.

  2. →
    Round 02 → reflection & ratification

    Once Round 02 closes, the bridging statements go to a face-to-face session at the School of Moral Ambition tax reform conference. The output is a public report — what the room agreed to, what's still contested, and what should happen next. Stages 03 and 04 of the Taiwan method, applied here.
