Why this exists

Democracy is slipping
almost everywhere.

Fig. ALiberal Democracy Index, 1990–2025

Liberal Democracy Index, 1990 to 2025. Hungary peaked at 0.78 in 2006 and collapsed to 0.28 in under two decades. Australia peaked at 0.86 in 2012. The question is whether the wobble is a plateau or the start of the same curve. Data: V‑Dem (2026), processed by Our World in Data. CC BY.

91 / 88

For the first time in twenty years, autocracies outnumber democracies. The gap opened in 2024.

~25%

of countries are now in active autocratisation — including, for the first time, the US, the UK, and Italy.

74%

of Australians prefer democracy to any other form of government, a record high. Our exception is real. It is also brittle.

Australia is, for now, an unusual exception to a global slide.1 We're holding the line — on democratic infrastructure that was built before the internet, while every comparable country slides past us. The digital tools that hollowed out public discourse can be turned the other way. Polis is one of them, used by vTaiwan and a dozen governments since to find consensus the noise was hiding.2

Field Notes / 01

How they did it
in Taiwan.

The method we use isn't ours. It was forged in Taipei after the 2014 Sunflower Movement, by a civic-tech crew called g0v and a digital minister named Audrey Tang. Over the past decade it has shaped real legislation, on ride-sharing, alcohol sales, fintech, and telemedicine, with a documented government action rate of around 80%. Here's how it works, and why it works.

On most contested issues, people aren't as divided as the loudest voices suggest. They're divided on some things and quietly agreed on others. Pol.is uses clustering to find those quiet agreements — the bridging statements — and surfaces them. The divisive stuff gets demoted. Outrage isn't rewarded.

Fig. 01 · How bridging works↓

Each dot is a participant. The two ellipses are opinion clusters discovered by the algorithm. A statement only one cluster agrees with (top) gets demoted. A statement both clusters agree with (bottom) gets surfaced. That's the bridge. Consensus is built from the bottom up, not negotiated top-down.

Fig. 02 · The four stages, unpacked

vTaiwan separates the work into four discrete stages, each with its own tools, participants, and outputs. Pol.is is one stage of four. The rest is human work — and most of it isn't online.

Case 01 / 2015 · The Uber question

Everyone really just
cared about safety.

1,700+Participants
4 → 2Cluster collapse
7Bridging statements
80%+Consensus threshold

When Uber arrived in 2015, taxi drivers were losing 30% of their income, no ministry wanted to touch it, and the company refused to engage. vTaiwan ran a Polis round. Within hours, four stakeholder groups collapsed into two camps screaming at each other — until people started writing statements both sides could live with. Seven crossed the 80% threshold. Audrey Tang took them into a live-streamed meeting with Uber, the taxi associations, and three ministries. Uber agreed to professional licenses, insurance disclosure, and transport taxes. Nobody walked in with that compromise. The algorithm found it.

A 13-year deadlock, broken in three weeks.

Case 02 / 2016 · The alcohol question

A deadlock
nobody could break.

~450Participants
13 yrsPrior deadlock

4Stakeholder groups

Taiwan banned online alcohol sales in 2003. By 2012 the Ministry of Finance wanted to lift the ban, but every attempt collapsed under the weight of who was in the room — retailers, e-commerce platforms, parents, public-health groups, all talking past each other. Thirteen years of deadlock. In 2016, the question went to vTaiwan. ~450 citizens voted over a few weeks. What emerged wasn't yes-or-no — it was yes, but: age verification, restricted delivery hours, ID at handover. None of those were anyone's opening position. They were the bridges. The new regulation reflected them almost exactly.

Field Notes / 02

Built slowly,
in public.

The Ministry of the Future is being built openly. Here's what we've done, what we're working on next, and what we're thinking about.

What we've done

  1. 01
    First prototype of the deliberation tool

    A standalone HTML build of a Polis-style tool with simulated participants voting on Australian infrastructure statements. Local-first, no backend. Built to understand the algorithm before deciding what to actually run with.

  2. 02
    Multi-user prototype on Supabase

    A second iteration with real-time voting across multiple people. Useful for understanding the cluster mechanics. Ultimately surfaced the lesson that the platform itself is the easy part, and the work is everything around it.

  3. 03
    The Ministry of the Future site

    This page. The project's public face, the Taiwan story, the case for why this work matters.

  4. 04
    Stakeholder & systems map

    A concentric-circle map of the Australian civic-tech and deliberative-democracy ecosystem: who's already doing this work, who could be allies, who sets the context.

  5. 05
    Round 01: wealth & intergenerational fairness → live

    Running on hosted pol.is, embedded above. In partnership with the Australian network of the School of Moral Ambition. The first real-stakes round, on the most chronically deadlocked policy question in Australian politics.

What's next

  1. →
    Crowdsourcing a regional festival

    Run a Polis round to help shape a small festival in central Victoria: music, water slides, the works. The kind of festival that could only exist because the people coming to it had a hand in shaping it. The next real-world use of the method, and a deliberately joyful one.

  2. →
    Round 01 → reflection & ratification

    Once Round 01 closes, the bridging statements go to a face-to-face session at the School of Moral Ambition tax reform conference. The output is a public report — what the room agreed to, what's still contested, and what should happen next. Stages 03 and 04 of the Taiwan method, applied here.

What we're thinking about

  1. ?
    Larger infrastructure deliberations

    High-speed rail, water security, energy grids, housing supply: the chronically deadlocked questions Australia famously can't decide on. Could the bridging method find the statements hiding underneath?

  2. ?
    Improving the platform itself

    Better clustering algorithms, a moderation queue for participant-proposed statements, mobile-friendly cluster maps, multi-round support, exportable session artefacts.

  3. ?
    Partnering with local councils

    Running rounds with the kinds of organisations that already have a mandate to consult their communities, and that could use a better tool for it than a paper survey or a town-hall meeting.

  4. ?
    Joining the global Polis community

    Connecting with the Computational Democracy Project, comparing notes with Decidim and Make.org, contributing back to the open-source ecosystem we're building on.
